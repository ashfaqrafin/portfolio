Every garment I've ever liked started as a boring rectangle with some curves on it. That rectangle is the block: a close-fitting base pattern, no style, no seam allowance, just you, translated into lines on paper.

Drafting one from scratch is humbling. It's also the single most useful thing I've done with a pencil.

<!--more-->

## Measure like you mean it

A block is only as honest as the numbers that go into it. I take every measurement twice, on different days, and average them, because bodies are not static and neither is my posture after lunch.

The core set I work from:

| Measurement | Where | What it controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chest | Fullest point, tape level | Overall width of the block |
| Waist | Natural waist, not where trousers sit | Dart intake and shaping |
| Back length | Nape to waist | Vertical balance |
| Across back | Between the arm creases | Shoulder blade ease |
| Shoulder | Neck point to shoulder point | Shoulder line slope and length |
| Neck | Base of the neck | Neckline curve |

A friend holding the tape helps. A mirror and stubbornness also works, just slower.

## Lines before curves

The drafting itself is mostly rectangles. I start with a vertical line for centre back, square across for the chest line, the waist line, and the hip line, then mark widths from my measurements plus ease.

1. Draw the frame: centre back, centre front, and the horizontal guides.
2. Divide the chest width into back, side, and front panels.
3. Plot the shoulder points and the neck points.
4. Connect them with the armhole and neckline curves.
5. Place the waist darts to take in the difference between chest and waist.

The curves are where it gets personal. An armhole drawn freehand will always look wrong to you and fine to everyone else. A set of French curves helps, and so does accepting that the first one is a draft.

## Ease is not a mistake

Early on I drafted a block to my exact measurements and produced a beautifully accurate garment I could not breathe in. Ease is the extra room that turns a diagram into clothing.

> A block with no ease is a portrait. A block with ease is a garment.

Small amounts go at the chest and waist, a little more across the back so you can reach forward. Too much and the block loses its point, which is to be the tightest true version of your shape.

## Test it in calico

Before any real cloth, the block goes into a toile: cheap calico, long stitches, no finishing. I put it on, pin out the wrinkles, and transfer every correction back to paper.

Drag lines point at the problem. Diagonal folds from the neck usually mean the shoulder slope is off. Gaping at the back armhole means too much width across the back.

The full piece is where the seam allowances live.

Only once the toile behaves do I add them, because adding allowance to a wrong line just gives you a wider wrong line. The block stays clean, net, and boring. That's the point. Everything interesting gets built on top of it.
