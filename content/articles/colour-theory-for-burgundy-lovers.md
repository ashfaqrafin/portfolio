I own more burgundy than is strictly reasonable. Knitwear, a chore coat, two pairs of socks I refuse to discuss. At some point a hobby becomes a palette, and a palette becomes a problem you have to solve on purpose.

This is my attempt to explain why burgundy works, where it falls apart, and how I pair it without looking like I've been upholstered.

<!--more-->

## What burgundy actually is

Burgundy is a red that has been pushed toward purple and then dragged down in value. That's the whole trick. It sits low and cool on the [colour wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_wheel), which is why it reads as serious rather than loud.

Three things to keep in mind:

- **Hue:** a red leaning violet. Lean too far and it turns aubergine; not far enough and it becomes brick.
- **Value:** dark, but not black-dark. It needs contrast to be seen at all.
- **Saturation:** moderate. A fully saturated burgundy looks like a stage curtain.

That low value is the important part. Burgundy behaves almost like a neutral, which means it can anchor an outfit instead of shouting over it.

## The pairings that earn their keep

I tested these the scientific way, which is to say by standing in front of a mirror and making faces.

| Partner colour | Why it works | Where it goes wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Navy | Two dark, cool tones that respect each other | Too close in value under dim light |
| Camel | Warm against cool, gentle contrast | Can look like a biscuit tin |
| Olive | Near-complement, muted enough to stay calm | Pick the wrong olive and it's army surplus |
| Grey | Lets burgundy be the only voice in the room | Flat grey makes everything look tired |
| Cream | Lifts the darkness, very clean | Pure white is too sharp; go off-white |

The rule hiding in that table: burgundy wants a partner that differs in *one* dimension, not three. Navy differs mostly in hue. Cream differs mostly in value. Olive is the brave one, sitting near the opposite side of the wheel but muted enough to keep the peace.

## Texture does half the work

Here's the thing nobody tells you. The same dye on different cloth is a different colour.

Burgundy on a flat worsted reads crisp and formal. On a brushed lambswool it goes soft and almost dusty. On corduroy the wale catches light on one side and swallows it on the other, so you get two burgundies for the price of one.

> If an outfit feels flat, change the fabric before you change the colour.

I've rescued more than one combination by swapping a smooth knit for a ribbed one and leaving the palette alone.

## A small, honest conclusion

Burgundy isn't hard. It's just particular. Treat it like a dark neutral, give it one point of contrast, and let texture handle the rest.

And yes, I am aware that the socks are a cry for help.
