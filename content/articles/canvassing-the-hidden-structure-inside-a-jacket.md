Cut open a well-made jacket (please don't, but imagine it) and you'll find a second garment hiding inside. It's a layer of canvas, shaped and stitched, holding the front of the jacket in a quiet, permanent curve.

That hidden layer is why some jackets drape and others just hang.

<!--more-->

## What the canvas does

The canvas sits between the outer cloth and the lining across the front of the jacket. It gives the chest its shape, keeps the front edge from collapsing, and lets the lapel roll instead of folding flat.

Traditionally it's a blend with some animal hair in it, which gives it spring. The hair wants to return to its shape, so the jacket does too.

There are a few ways to build a jacket front:

- **Fused:** an adhesive interlining bonded to the cloth with heat. Quick and consistent, but stiffer, and it can bubble over time.
- **Half-canvas:** a floating canvas through the chest and lapel, fusing below. A sensible compromise.
- **Full canvas:** a floating canvas the full length of the front. Slow, heavy on handwork, and the one I keep coming back to.

## Building the chest piece

A full canvas isn't one layer. It's a little stack:

1. The main canvas, cut to the front panel shape, with darts to shape it over the chest.
2. A chest piece of firmer haircloth, laid over the upper chest for extra body.
3. A soft felt or domette on top, so the stiff layers don't show through the cloth.

I baste the stack together, then shape it over a tailor's ham with steam. It already wants to be a chest before it ever meets the jacket.

## Pad stitching, or meditation with a needle

The lapel area gets pad stitched: rows of small diagonal stitches through the canvas, catching only a thread or two of the outer cloth. I roll the lapel over my fingers while I stitch, so the canvas is trained into a curve.

> Pad stitching is the slowest way to make something look effortless.

The stitches shouldn't show on the face. If they do, I've caught too much cloth. If the lapel goes flat when I let go, I haven't rolled it enough. It takes hours and there's no shortcut I've found that gives the same result.

## Why bother

Because a canvassed front moves with you. It shapes itself to your chest over time, a bit like breaking in a pair of shoes. Fused fronts stay exactly as they left the press, which is fine until it isn't.

It also makes the jacket repairable. A floating canvas can be adjusted, re-pressed, and coaxed back into shape. Glue tends to be a one-way decision.

None of this shows from the outside. That's sort of the whole appeal. The structure is invisible, and the only evidence it exists is that the jacket looks like it's been waiting for you.
