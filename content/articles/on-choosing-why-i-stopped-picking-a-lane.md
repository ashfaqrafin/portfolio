"Pick a lane" is good advice for driving. As career advice, I've found it less useful, mostly because I kept failing at it.

I'd commit to code and find myself sketching lapels in the margins. I'd commit to tailoring and catch myself writing a script to calculate dart intake. Eventually I stopped fighting it.

<!--more-->

## The case for picking a lane

I want to be fair to the advice, because it isn't wrong. Focus compounds. Depth takes time. Spreading yourself across three crafts means you'll be slower at each of them than someone who picked one.

There's also a clarity to it. A single lane is easy to explain at parties. "I tailor" lands. "I tailor, and also code, and also have strong opinions about shell prompts" gets a polite nod and a refill.

So I tried. Several times. Each attempt went roughly the same way:

1. Declare a focus with great seriousness.
2. Feel productive for a while.
3. Notice the other crafts creeping back in through the side door.
4. Feel guilty about it.

## What I noticed instead

The crafts weren't competing. They were cross-pollinating.

- Pattern drafting taught me to respect structure before decoration.
- The terminal taught me to make my work repeatable and inspectable.
- Fashion taught me that proportion and rhythm are felt before they're understood.

Every one of those lessons showed up in the other crafts. The tailoring made me a more careful programmer. The programming made me a more systematic tailor. Fashion made both of them less boring.

> A lane is a direction. It doesn't have to be a wall.

## Choosing, differently

I don't think the answer is "do everything." That way lies a lot of half-finished jackets and abandoned repos. I have both. I'm not proud.

What I do now is choose by project instead of by identity. This month, the focus is this coat. This week, this tool. The lanes stay open, but I'm only driving in one at a time.

That small shift took the guilt out of it. I'm not failing to commit. I'm committing, repeatedly, to whatever is in front of me.

## The sequel to the day and night shift

When I wrote about shipping code by day and tailoring by night, I thought I was describing a balance. Now I think it was a merge. The two shifts were never separate; they were one craft with two sets of tools.

So I've stopped picking a lane. I've picked a way of working instead: careful, structural, a bit obsessive about fit. It travels well. It works on cloth, on code, and on whatever I end up curious about next.
